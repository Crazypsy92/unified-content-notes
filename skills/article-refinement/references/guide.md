# Article Refinement Reference Guide

This guide combines the rules needed by the standalone article-refinement skill. The sources at the end provide general writing principles; local university, supervisor, journal, and publisher requirements take precedence.

## Academic Writing

### Make claims traceable

A strong academic paragraph normally makes clear what object or problem is discussed, what evidence supports the claim, what inference is drawn, and what scope or limitation applies. Do not force every paragraph into a rigid template.

### Prefer precision over inflated language

Replace vague praise with observable details. Instead of saying a method is “highly efficient and has broad application prospects,” state which operation it improves, under what conditions, and what evidence supports the claim. If evidence is not available, describe the point as an objective or expectation, not a result.

### Match claim strength to evidence

Distinguish established knowledge, findings actually observed in the user's work, planned work and hypotheses, interpretations, and unresolved questions. Do not turn “may”, “is expected to”, or “will be evaluated” into “has achieved”.

### Build coherent paragraphs

A paragraph should usually develop one main point. Transitions should express a real logical relation—contrast, cause, sequence, or qualification—not merely decorate the prose. Split paragraphs when the subject or reasoning changes.

### Handle literature responsibly

A literature review should synthesize what sources actually study, how they study it, what they find, and what remains unresolved. Do not invent a research gap or cite a paper for a claim it does not support.

### Academic integrity

The author remains responsible for the research question, methods, evidence, analysis, and conclusions. AI may assist with language review and organization, but must not fabricate data, citations, authorship, or work performed. Follow the applicable institutional and publication disclosure rules.

## Revision and Fidelity

### Minimal-edit hierarchy

Apply the smallest intervention that solves the problem:

1. Delete genuinely redundant words or repeated information.
2. Repair grammar, punctuation, ambiguity, or sentence order.
3. Clarify the relationship between clauses or sentences.
4. Split an overloaded sentence or paragraph.
5. Rebuild a paragraph only when its reasoning cannot be followed.
6. Recommend structural changes separately; do not silently rewrite the whole section.

If the original is already clear and accurate, retain it.

### Protected content

Unless the user explicitly asks to change them, preserve technical terms, definitions, abbreviations, symbols, equations, variable names, values, ranges, units, dates, model numbers, experimental conditions, citation markers, reference numbers, author-year citations, footnotes, bibliography order, figure/table/equation numbering, scope limitations, assumptions, uncertainty, and future-work statements.

If a parameter or claim appears inconsistent, flag it instead of silently correcting it.

### Three levels of intervention

- **Language issue:** correct directly when meaning is unambiguous.
- **Reasoning issue:** explain the gap and offer a revision using only information already present.
- **Factual or research-design issue:** mark “作者确认” and ask for evidence; never fill it with a plausible guess.

### Contextual review of vague or AI-like writing

Do not use a forbidden-word blacklist. A phrase is problematic only when it is empty, repetitive, exaggerated, disconnected from evidence, or inconsistent with the author's actual work. Common patterns worth reviewing include generic background openings, repeated claims of significance, broad words such as “systematic”, “comprehensive”, “intelligent”, or “efficient” without operational meaning, unsupported novelty claims, and plans written as completed results. Ordinary academic transitions are not automatically wrong.

### Author voice

If a writing sample is available, use it to infer sentence length, formality, paragraph rhythm, and preferred terminology. Without a sample, use restrained, readable academic Chinese. Do not invent the author's experiences, emotions, or motivations.

## Genre Rules

### Thesis and proposal

Separate research objectives, research contents, methods, technical route, innovation points, and expected outcomes. Check that each objective has a corresponding method and a feasible verification path. Do not overstate novelty, feasibility, sample availability, laboratory resources, or expected performance.

### Literature review

For each cited work, identify only supported details: problem, method, data or scenario, result, and limitation. Group studies by technical route or research question rather than listing disconnected abstracts. Keep citations attached to the claims they support.

### Technical documents and engineering reports

Prioritize reproducibility. Preserve versions, hardware models, software versions, pin names, units, interface definitions, protocol details, test conditions, and measured results. Separate tested facts from assumptions, proposals, and unverified claims.

### Work reports

Separate planned work, completed work, blockers, measured outcomes, and next steps. Do not turn an intention into a completion claim.

### General essays

Preserve the author's thesis and tone. Improve paragraph focus, transitions, and concrete examples. Do not force academic formality onto personal reflections or creative prose.

## Document and QA

### DOCX preservation

Work on a copy. Preserve where possible section breaks, page layout, margins, headers, footers, page numbers, heading styles, multilevel numbering, captions, cross-references, tables, images, charts, equations, text boxes, hyperlinks, footnotes, endnotes, comments, tracked changes, and EndNote or other reference-manager fields.

Do not claim preservation unless the output has been checked. If an editing method may flatten citation fields or alter layout, warn the user before making destructive changes.

### Reference integrity

Do not reorder references, renumber citations, or convert dynamic citation fields to plain text unless explicitly requested. Spot-check that in-text citation markers still map to the intended bibliography entries.

### Final QA

- [ ] The revision answers the requested task and scope.
- [ ] No data, sources, equipment, experiments, or personal experiences were invented.
- [ ] Technical terms, numbers, units, equations, and identifiers remain unchanged unless approved.
- [ ] Planned work and completed work remain distinct.
- [ ] Citation markers and bibliography order remain intact.
- [ ] Headings, figure/table numbers, and cross-references remain coherent.
- [ ] No paragraph is more certain than its evidence permits.
- [ ] The revised text remains understandable and defensible by the author.
- [ ] Unverified document-layout details are disclosed.
